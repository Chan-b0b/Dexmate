Vision-language-action (VLA) policies increasingly receive wrist wrench and contact signals in
their state, yet a behavior-cloned policy given the full wrench can still fail to bind its
behavior to it. On a real-robot suction picking task with a force-sensitive payload---a hard
contact-force limit, so that force must govern when the descent ends---a naively fine-tuned
VLA shows the familiar failure: the wrench in its state does not come to govern its behavior,
and it presses on through contact until an external safety limit intervenes. We present a conditioning pathway that changes how force is delivered, not what is
delivered: a condition vector \chat{} (contact, force magnitude, seal) computed from signals the
policy already receives and fed to the VLA through a FiLM layer. The pathway has three
properties. It is \emph{drop-in}: no backbone change, no new information, no cost in imitation
error, and, since it touches only the state input, it applies to any VLA that consumes a
proprioceptive state. It is
\emph{retrofittable}: attached to an already-trained naive policy and trained further on the
same demonstrations, it rewires how that policy responds to force. And it \emph{generalizes
beyond the demonstrations}: because force magnitude reaches the action through FiLM's affine
modulation, the policy brakes
harder the harder it is pressed, stopping and retreating at forces beyond the demonstrations,
where imitation alone leaves the response unspecified; and because the stop is tied to force
rather than to appearance, picks succeed at a stack height absent from training. We also ran it on our robot: every
FiLM-policy pick stops on its own at low contact force, whereas the naive policy drives contact
force past the limit in every attempt, and on-robot counterfactual probes reproduce the offline
crossover.
